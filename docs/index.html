People talking, sharing and governing themselves, with nobody in the middle.

This is the kernel of the CIRIS constitution: the one function a group’s own devices run to decide what their signed statements mean, published with its proofs.

One ten-person vote, three ways

A neighbourhood club of ten, a co-op board or an open-source team has a shared budget and has to choose how to spend it. Here is where the ballots travel, and who holds the power, in each of three ways of running that vote.

Hosted app

How you vote

You tap an option in a poll in a hosted chat or forms service. Your ballot goes to that company’s server, which keeps the list of who may vote and does the count.

Where the power sits

With whoever runs the server. They can change the roll, close the poll, read answers that were meant to be private, or lose everything if the service goes away or bans the group.

How it settles

The server announces the result. Members see what it shows them and have nothing of their own to recount.

What it costs you

It is convenient, and often costs nothing in money. The group’s record and its privacy sit with the operator.

Blockchain

How you vote

Your ballot is a transaction sent to a public blockchain. Many unrelated validators order transactions into one shared ledger, and the vote is counted from what the ledger holds.

Where the power sits

No single server holds it. The ordering is done by validators the group does not choose, and the group depends on infrastructure far larger than itself.

How it settles

When the network has ordered the transactions and treats them as final, by that chain’s own rule. Anyone can then read the result from the ledger.

What it costs you

Each voter needs tokens and pays a fee for each transaction. On most public chains who voted, and often how, is public and permanent.

CIRIS kernel

How you vote

Your phone signs a ballot row and passes it to the other members’ devices. Ballots can arrive late or out of order, and every phone computes the same result from the same ballots.

Proved in Causal.lean; run over every arrival order by check.py.

Where the power sits

Only with the ten members: there is no server in the middle and no global ledger. The roll is the group’s own roster at the head the vote names, readable by every member, and no one admitted later changes who was eligible. One person is one voice however many devices they have, and a person whose ballots disagree has none.

Members at a head: RosterHead.lean.

How it settles

An open vote is final once its count is met: a count once met is final, and anyone holding the rows can recount it.

A secret vote: each member’s choice is split between two members drawn from the roster, each adds up what it received, and only the total is published, above a minimum number of voters. A member sends the same amount of data whether or not a vote is open, so an outsider cannot tell that one is being held.

A count once met is final: Consent.lean. The secret tally is model-checked in the parent specification: formal/census_ballot.

What it costs you

No fees, no tokens, no servers to rent: the devices and connections people already have.

  • Hosted appTrust the company’s server.
  • BlockchainPay the validators.
  • CIRISRun the count on your own phone.

What this does not do

  • That each key is one living person is the group’s own admission; the kernel does not establish it.
  • A secret ballot’s secrecy assumes the two members who tally it do not both collude.
  • Nothing stops a voter who is watched or paid from voting as told, and nothing protects a vote cast from a device someone else controls.
  • Members who withhold can stop a count from being met, though not falsify one.

It is a solution for a body that admits its own members: a family, a co-op, a union, a board, a neighbourhood, an online community. It is not shown for a public election. The full statement is in the README, Voting over the internet.

What it is for

Your things on your devices

Your files live on the devices you own and copy between them. A family’s files live on the family’s devices. No provider is needed to say which copy is right.

Rooms without a host

A room’s members, its keys and its moderators are signed rows the members hold. A live video call or a group chat runs between the members’ devices; no service in the middle admits people, holds the key or keeps the recording.

Decisions that are the group’s

A vote no single voice can carry, a moderator the members can recall, a removal that takes a count: each is a row signed by enough of the people it binds, and anyone holding it can check it.

AI agents that answer to people

An agent acts under a named person’s consent. What it cannot undo, it does only with the acknowledgement of those who could have stopped it. A halt reaches every node that receives it, and nothing an agent holds can disarm one in advance: the parent text calls the halt the condition of symmetry between makers and what they make (GOVERNANCE.md).

The screen is the same function

A screen is a function of the rows a node holds, the viewer’s key and the clock: the same inputs as the kernel. So what a person is offered, what an act would change, and whether the network admits it are one answer, not a view kept in step with a model.

A person is never shown a button the network would refuse, or a state that only their own device believes.

Proved over one abstract family (formal/event_lifecycle, in the parent specification), and run over the specification’s own tables and the kernel’s own rows, with planted breaks caught (formal/screen). The vocabulary is 24 screen primitives, a draft standard: ui_primitives.json. The limit is measured too: twenty-five ways back that the text states have no transition in the table, so no screen built from the table can offer them.

Under the hood

Three kinds of finality, the say rule, checkpoint and purge

For any set of signed rows a node holds, and every later set containing them, every answer the kernel gives is one of three kinds.

Final by form
Whether a row is kept; its conclusion over its own causal past; a row that names a blocking set of receipts. (Causal.lean, Settled.lean)
Final by acknowledgement
Whether an act counts at the head it names, once n − t + 1 of the n members at that head have given clean receipts, where t can cut it. The threshold is exact in both directions. This is finality of an act against later cuts, a different thing from a vote’s count being met. (Settled.lean)
Provisional
Everything else, including whether an act is still in force at a later head. A voice seated later acts forward, never backwards. (Settled.lean)
The say rule
A voice that has both acknowledged an act and voted to cut it, in rows neither of which names the other, has no say there. It is forced by symmetry and closure. (Settled.lean)
Checkpoint and purge
A node may keep its folded state, sign a checkpoint under its own key, and drop the bytes of settled acts beneath it; it keeps each dropped row’s digest, what it names and its conclusion. None of this is a duty. Dropping an unsettled act’s rows is proved unsafe by an exhibited witness pair (Settled.lean), and purging is run against keeping everything, with eight planted breaks caught (check_purge.py). The least set of rows a node must keep is open.

Where it comes from

The kernel is what results when a relational ontology is applied to ethics. The parent text’s first commitment is that a person is a person through other persons. Taken as engineering, nothing in the system has a state of its own: every fact about a person, a device, a group or an authority is a signed relation between keys. Standing is then consent between those it binds, authority is never a list, and the function over those relations is the kernel. The ethics came first; the mechanism is what it required.

The arithmetic is classical. Quorum intersection is Gifford, “Weighted Voting for Replicated Data” (SOSP ’79); the same argument where some voices sign both ways is Malkhi and Reiter, “Byzantine Quorum Systems” (Distributed Computing 11(4), 1998). Finality from a locally checkable set of signed acknowledgements, with no total order, is the approach of Guerraoui, Kuznetsov, Monti, Pavlovič and Seredinschi, “The Consensus Number of a Cryptocurrency” (PODC ’19). The split between answers that only grow and answers a later row can change is Hellerstein and Alvaro, “Keeping CALM” (CACM 63(9), 2020). The closure normal form behind the checkpoint, that a view is closed under adding rows exactly when no witness pair exists, is a standard factorisation argument restated in Settled.lean. None of the pieces is new. What is here is the four constraints met together, for the acts by which people share, admit, decide and remove, in one function with its proofs: the application of those results to a governance grammar of signed rows, and the rules that application forces.
